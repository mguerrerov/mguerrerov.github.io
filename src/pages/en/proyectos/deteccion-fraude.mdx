## What problem it solves

In this dataset fraud is 0.17% of the transactions. That imbalance is the whole
problem, and it breaks the first thing anyone looks at: a model that always answers
"legitimate", without looking at anything, is right 99.83% of the time. Overall accuracy
says nothing here, and using it as the criterion leads straight to a useless model.

What has to be decided is what to do about that disproportion. There are three common
answers and it is not obvious which one wins, so I tried all three against two models and
compared the six results with metrics that do tell them apart.

## Methodology

Variables `V1` to `V28` come already transformed by PCA from the source, so only
`Amount` and `Time` need scaling. I removed 1,081 duplicate rows and split 80/20
stratified, so that the fraud proportion is the same in training and in test: without
stratifying, an unlucky random split can leave almost all the fraud on one side.

On that set, three strategies:

- **SMOTE**, which synthesises new fraudulent transactions by interpolating between the
  real ones, until the classes are balanced.
- **Undersampling**, which discards legitimate transactions until they match. The
  training set ends up at 756 rows, 378 per class.
- **`class_weight='balanced'`**, which does not touch the data and merely penalises
  errors on the minority class more heavily.

And two models: logistic regression, as a baseline, and random forest, because a space
transformed by PCA can have non-linear separations that a linear model cannot reach.

## Results

<Tabla idioma="en" />

Random forest wins without touching the data: an F1 of 0.8364 and a precision of 98.57%.
The interesting part is that the two resampling techniques, the ones usually cited as
*the* solution to imbalance, come out behind simply reweighting the loss function.

The bottom four rows show why recall alone is worth nothing. They all beat 86% recall and
they all have a precision below 8%: they find the fraud because they flag almost
everything as fraud. In production that means blocking thousands of legitimate purchases
to catch a few fraudulent ones, which is an expensive way to fail.

## Decisions and trade-offs

### Comparing the three strategies instead of picking one

I could have read that SMOTE is the recommended thing, applied it and presented the
result. It would have given an F1 of 0.8324, which sounds fine until you see that doing
nothing at all gives 0.8364 with less work and without inventing data.

Every technique has its risk and none of them is theoretical: SMOTE introduces noise near
the decision boundary, because the transactions it manufactures never existed;
undersampling throws away real data and leaves the training set at 756 rows out of the
283,726 available. Measuring them was the only way to know which one weighed more here.

### Random forest and logistic regression, not just one

Logistic regression is the mandatory baseline: if a linear model is enough, everything
else is superfluous. It was not enough, and not by a small margin — it never got past an
F1 of 0.11 in any configuration.

That difference is a result in itself. It says that the separation between fraud and
non-fraud in this space is not linear, and that the combinations of variables a tree finds
are doing work no straight boundary can do.

### F1 and AUC, never accuracy

F1 combines precision and recall at the default threshold, and AUC measures the quality of
the ordering across every threshold at once. Between them they cover the two questions
that matter: whether the individual alerts are reliable, and whether the model knows how
to rank by risk even if the cut-off is set wrong.

## Limitations I have not solved

### The train/test split leaks the future into the past

It is the most serious methodological flaw in the project and it is not fixed. I split the
transactions at random, so the training set contains operations later than those in the
test set. A deployed model never has that: it only sees the past.

The correct split would be by time, training on the first months and evaluating on the
last ones. The metrics would very likely drop, because fraud patterns change and a random
split makes it easy on the model. The figures on this page have to be read knowing this.

### The threshold stayed at 0.5

Every model classifies at the default threshold, which is rarely the optimal one. The
precision-recall curve is computed and it shows where a reasonable cut-off would be
depending on what each type of error costs, but I never got round to moving it.

It is the pending improvement with the best effort-to-result ratio: it requires no
retraining, only deciding which hurts more, a false alarm or a fraud that gets through.
