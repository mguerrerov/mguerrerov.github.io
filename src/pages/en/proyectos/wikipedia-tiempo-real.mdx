## What problem it solves

Wikimedia publishes every change on every wiki in an open stream, with no authentication,
as soon as it happens. I wanted to use it to answer three questions: how much activity
each wiki has minute by minute, what share of the changes is made by bots and what share
by people, and which pages are being edited by more than one person **at once**.

All three can be answered with a batch process running once a day, and all three would
come out wrong. The second one proves it: measured on real data, the bot share swung
between 27.8% and 35.2% across seven windows of a single run, and an earlier capture in
another time slot had given 41.1%. A single number per day averages that variation until
it disappears, and the answer with it.

The third one cannot even be formulated in batch. "At once" is a property of the window,
not of the day.

The second goal was for the pipeline to run in two places without forking: on a laptop
with Docker Compose and on AWS over Kinesis and EMR Serverless. The rule I set myself was
that not a single `if entorno == "aws"` should appear inside the jobs. I nearly broke it
without noticing, and that is further down.

## Decisions and trade-offs

### The event time is `meta.dt`, not `timestamp`

Every event carries two timestamps. `timestamp` is when the change happened in MediaWiki,
and it is the one that intuitively looks right for grouping into windows.

I measured both on a ten-minute capture before writing anything. `timestamp` arrives out of
order 62.35% of the time, with a maximum delay of **19.3 years**: they are `log` events
from Commons, where that field holds the original date of the file, not that of the
change. `meta.dt` — when the bus published it — is out of order 1.28% of the time, with a
maximum of 0.99 s.

With `timestamp` as the time axis, any reasonable watermark would have silently discarded
part of the traffic, and the pipeline would have looked healthy. I use `meta.dt` and keep
`timestamp` as a business column. The cost is that the windows measure when Wikimedia
published the change and not when it happened: 1.7 s of difference at the median.

### Bronze stores the JSON unparsed

The entry layer writes the event as text, exactly as it arrived, plus the metadata of the
queue envelope: partition, offset and timestamp.

It is more expensive on disk than typing it from the start, and I do it anyway for two
reasons. The first is that if Wikimedia changes the schema tomorrow, bronze keeps ingesting
and the problem is solved in silver with the raw data still available. The second is that
`(partition, offset)` uniquely identifies each message, and the offsets of a partition are
consecutive: that makes it possible to prove that a restart lost nothing and duplicated
nothing with an **exact** method, not an approximate one. Over two runs with a stop and a
start in between, 25,911 rows, zero duplicates and zero gaps.

### A 30-second watermark

The source's disorder is below one second, so 30 s is two orders of magnitude of margin. I
did not set it for the disorder: I set it for what can really delay an event, which is a
producer reconnection or a Spark consumption backlog.

I validated it by injecting deliberately delayed events with a synthetic generator. Up to
30 s of delay, 100% survives; between 30 and 45 s, 93.6%; between 45 and 60 s, 10.1%;
above 60 s none survives. The cut does not fall cleanly at second 30 because the watermark
only advances between micro-batches, so the effective threshold is 30 s plus however long
the batch in progress takes. That transition band is the correct behaviour, not a defect
of the measurement.

I also deduplicate within that window instead of remembering every identifier seen. The
version without a watermark keeps every `meta.id` **forever**: the state grows without
bound and the job ends up dying of memory. The price is that a duplicate more than 30 s
away from its original is not detected, and I accept that because duplicates come from
reconnections and reprocessing, which happen within seconds.

### A sliding window only for the concurrent pages

The first two questions use a fixed one-minute window. The third uses a five-minute
sliding window advancing every minute, and it costs five times as much state.

With a fixed window, two people editing the same page at 10:00:59 and at 10:01:01 fall
into different windows and the coincidence does not show up. Which is exactly what the
question is trying to detect.

### One provisioned Kinesis shard, not on-demand

On-demand mode scales by itself and charges a considerably higher hourly base fee. It pays
off with irregular or large traffic, and here the throughput is measured and stable: 37.4
ev/s on average, a peak of 114, around 53 KB/s. One shard takes 1 MB/s, twenty times what
is needed.

If the throughput shot up, shards would have to be added by hand. With this source that is
not going to happen.

## What broke and how I fixed it

### It worked locally and would not have started on AWS

This is the mistake I learned the most from, and it gave no symptom at all.

I had written an abstraction over the event source so the job would not know whether it
was reading from Kafka or from Kinesis. It gave Spark the format and the read options, and
that seemed enough to me. It was not: Kafka returns columns `key, value, topic, partition,
offset, timestamp` and Kinesis returns `data, streamName, partitionKey, sequenceNumber,
approximateArrivalTimestamp`. And `bronce.py` was doing `F.col("offset")`.

There was no `if entorno == "aws"` anywhere. The environment difference had crept in all
the same, column by column, underneath the abstraction that existed to prevent it. Locally
it worked perfectly.

I found out by reading the EMR documentation before writing the Terraform, not by running
anything on AWS. The abstraction moved on to normalising the whole envelope to a common
schema, not just supplying the format. The cost is that `desplazamiento` had to become
text, because the Kinesis sequence number has 56 digits and does not fit in a `BIGINT`, so
the gap count needs a `CAST` and only applies to Kafka.

The lesson I take away: an abstraction that has not been exercised against both
implementations is not tested, only written.

### A JAR that existed and still would not load

The job died when creating the Spark session with `UnsupportedClassVersionError`, class
file 61.0 against a runtime that recognised up to 55.0.

I had verified that the Iceberg artifact existed for my Spark version and my Scala
version. It does. What I had not looked at is the third axis: class file 61 is Java 17, and
the short tags of the `apache/spark` image ship Java 11. That a JAR exists for your Spark
and your Scala does not mean it works with your Java.

The solution was the long tag of the same Spark version, which changes only the runtime. I
ruled out dropping Iceberg to a version compiled for Java 11: it ties the project to an
older dependency to fix a problem that is not in that dependency. As a side effect, it
ended up aligned with Corretto 17, which is what EMR 7.x uses.

### I pinned the versions looking at the laptop instead of looking at the target

The first version matrix put Spark 3.5.9 and Iceberg 1.11.0, which was the most recent
released. On checking the AWS documentation it turned out that EMR Serverless 7.13.0 ships
Spark 3.5.6 and Iceberg 1.10.0.

I dropped the local environment to those two. It is easier to drop a version on the laptop
than to argue with AWS, and the goal of the project was for the jobs to be identical in
both environments. Hadoop and Scala did not change, so the adjustment touched only two
pieces.

I took the chance to verify the seven JARs one by one with a request to Maven Central,
instead of taking them for granted. Two of them were transitive dependencies of the Kafka
connector that I had not declared: without them the job starts and fails on reading, which
is the most expensive way to find out that a file is missing.

### The checkpoints took up thirteen times more than the data

I went to measure how much the pipeline took up on disk in order to size S3. The three
data layers came to 13.7 MB. The checkpoint directory, **180 MB**.

It is not a bug: Spark keeps by default the last hundred versions of the state of each
query, and here there are five stateful queries, three of them with windowed aggregation.
What interested me is the consequence, which goes against intuition: sizing storage by the
data volume leaves out precisely the part that takes up the most. Lowering
`minBatchesToRetain` before pushing this to AWS is still pending.

### The synthetic generator contaminated the answer

I had used the generator to provoke delays and duplicates the real source does not
produce. On looking at the concurrent-pages table, it was full of invented titles: 400
titles and 60 synthetic users generate far more coincidence than real traffic. And the
generator produces 41% bots by construction, so it had also dirtied the human/bot share.

The easy option was to filter the synthetic events by their comment inside the gold jobs. I
ruled it out: that puts test logic into production code. The generator's trials moved to
running over disposable volumes, and before any run meant for measuring, `docker compose
down -v` and start clean with the real source only.

Redoing the ingestion costs about ten minutes. That is cheap next to publishing a
contaminated number. The earlier figures were marked as unusable rather than deleted.

### A latency measurement that measured something else

The first end-to-end measurement gave a p50 of 106.84 s and struck me as enormous.

It was, because silver was catching up on the history accumulated in bronze while I was
measuring: what I was timing was the catch-up speed, not the latency of the pipeline in
motion. Repeated with the three layers started in a staggered way and with no accumulated
backlog, over 18,556 rows, the p50 came out at 15.59 s and the p95 at 55.75 s.

The p50 fits the theoretical floor: two chained ten-second triggers, one in bronze and one
in silver. The tail out to 82 s is events arriving just after a micro-batch closes and
waiting for the next one in each of the two layers. Lowering it is a matter of shortening
the triggers, at the cost of more small files in Iceberg, and I have not touched it because
15 s is more than enough for the questions this pipeline asks.

I left the bad measurement documented next to the good one, marked as discarded. The
mistake is more instructive than the number.
