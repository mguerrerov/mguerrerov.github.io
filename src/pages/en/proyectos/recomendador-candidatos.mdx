## What problem it solves

A keyword filter discards anyone who writes the same thing in different words. A CV that
says "ETL pipeline design" does not show up in a search for "data engineering", even
though it is exactly what is being looked for.

Semantic closeness solves that: it turns the CV and the offer into vectors and measures
the angle between them, so two texts about the same subject end up close even if they
share no vocabulary. But it brings its own problem, which is that it ignores whatever is
not semantic. A candidate with two years of experience can look extremely similar to an
offer asking for five.

That is why the system makes two passes. First it ranks by pure similarity. Then it
deducts positions for the hard requirements it knows how to read: years of experience,
degree and English level. The original score is left untouched and stored alongside the
adjusted one, so it is possible to see what the filter moved and why.

Everything runs locally, with no calls to paid APIs.

## Decisions and trade-offs

### A multilingual model, even though it is worse in English

`all-MiniLM-L6-v2` scores better in pure English, and it was the default option. Real CVs
mix Spanish and English on the same page, sometimes on the same line, and a monolingual
model does not put both languages in the same space: "aprendizaje automático" and
"machine learning" would end up far apart.

I used `paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2`, which aligns the two languages at the cost
of some accuracy in each one separately. For this case the bilingualism weighs more than
the ceiling.

### MySQL with a JSON column, not a vector database

With 18 candidates, a vector index speeds up nothing: the exhaustive search goes through
18 vectors of 384 dimensions and finishes before an approximate index would have been
built.

I store the serialised vector in a `JSON` column of MySQL 8, which has no native vector
type, and compute the similarity in Python. It is a decision about scale, not about
ignorance: past hundreds of thousands of CVs the right answer would be pgvector or Qdrant,
and this whole layer would have to be redone.

### The cosine written by hand

`sklearn.metrics.pairwise.cosine_similarity` solves it in one line. I wrote it with numpy
so the formula is in plain sight and can be audited when a result looks odd, which in a
ranking system happens often. It is ten lines and depends on nothing.

### Three states in the filter, not two

A requirement can be met, not met, or **unknown**. The extraction is done with regular
expressions and it fails: out of the 18 candidates, it gets the years of experience for 17
and the degree for 16.

If the filter were binary, an extractor failure would penalise someone for a problem in
the program, not theirs. With the third state, what is unknown does not deduct. And
whoever does not meet a requirement is not excluded but pushed downwards with the reason
written beside it: a visible error can be corrected, one that erases a candidate from the
list cannot.

## What broke and how I fixed it

### Long CVs scored high against any offer

The model only looks at 128 tokens at a time, so the CV is chopped up and the vectors of
the fragments are averaged. The average of a long, varied CV falls near the centre of the
semantic space, and from the centre everything is at middling distance. A generalist
profile came out second or third in offers where it was not the best candidate.

I tried the obvious alternative: scoring by the best-matching fragment instead of
averaging. I measured both with two negative controls, CVs that should not appear near the
top in any offer, and compared their average position over 18 candidates. The plain
average left them at 16.62; scoring by the best fragment raised them to 13.88, that is,
higher than they should be.

That measurement comes from an earlier version of the system, with four shorter offers and
no requirement filter, so its numbers do not compare with the rest of this page. What
settled the question was the difference between the two options, not their absolute value.

The alternative was worse, so I kept the average and left the limitation documented
instead of hiding it. It is still there.

### The middle of the ranking means nothing

Between positions 8 and 15 the cosine differences drop below 0.02. At that distance the
order is noise: reordering those candidates answers to nothing measurable.

I have not fixed it, because I do not think it can be fixed with this approach. What I did
was stop treating the ranking as a list ordered top to bottom and consider it reliable
only at the extremes.

### A scanned CV came in as an empty candidate

A PDF that is an image yields no text, and the pipeline indexed it all the same: a
candidate with a vector built on an empty string, taking up a place in the ranking.

I did not add OCR, which was a lot of work for an infrequent case. The extractor detects
that it has not pulled out any useful text and rejects the file explicitly. A candidate
who is missing and says so is preferable to one who is there and is a lie.
