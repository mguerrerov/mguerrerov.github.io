## What problem it solves

I wanted to answer three questions about public GitHub activity: what share of pull
requests is generated by bots and AI agents, and how that proportion changes over time;
how long a PR takes to get a review or a merge, and whether the clock runs differently for
a bot than for a person; which repositories are gaining or losing active contributors. The
GitHub API is no use for that, because it returns today's state and what is needed here is
long series.

GH Archive publishes the historical dump of public events: the complete record, not a
sample. In exchange it arrives in an awkward format. One compressed file per hour, one by
one, and inside a JSON whose payload does not always carry the same thing. On 9 October
2025 the full payload stopped being published, without warning, and between the 9th and
the 14th the volume collapsed and did not recover until the 15th. Any query crossing that
boundary has to work with both schemas.

The backfill runs on a desktop computer, a Ryzen 7 5800X with 31.9 GB of RAM. The initial
reconnaissance and the bisection that pinned down the format change were done on free
GitHub Actions runners, because for a while the local network could not reach the source.
No paid cloud: the cost is 0 euros.

I set myself a budget of 250 GB (232.8 GiB) for the history. It was not the machine's
limit, which had 1,378 GB free on another disk, but a restriction I imposed so as not to
dedicate the whole computer to it. Within that budget, the disk filled up before the CPU
did, and that decided the codec, the time window and the order of operations.

## Decisions and trade-offs

### I swapped snappy for zstd

I started with snappy, the codec Spark uses by default when writing Parquet, and I had no
reason to doubt it. Measuring one full day (2025-08-13, 3,794,323 events) the Parquet
weighed 3.599 GiB: 1.788 times the source `.gz`. I was paying disk to convert to columnar
and on top of that ending up with files larger than the ones I had downloaded.

I repeated the measurement with zstd on the same day: 1.723 GiB, a ratio of 0.856×, and
135.0 seconds of writing against snappy's 222.9. The 52% smaller size was expected; that
it was also faster was not, because the usual argument in favour of snappy is precisely
speed.

In exchange, zstd decompresses somewhat more slowly on repeated reads. With this usage
profile — written once, read in batches — that does not offset what it saves. With cheap
storage and interactive queries I would have decided the other way round.

### Projecting the JSON on only five event types

Storing bronze as it comes, with the whole JSON as a text column, was the comfortable
option: bronze is supposed to be raw and that way I closed no doors. It is what I did at
first.

My three questions only touch five event types. I projected the full JSON on those five
alone and discarded it for the rest. The same day dropped from 1.723 GiB to 1.078 GiB, an
additional 37% on top of what zstd had already gained.

If tomorrow I want to look at the commit array of a `PushEvent`, the payload is no longer
there and those days have to be downloaded again. I accepted the dependency because the
source is still published and because without the trimming the 12.5 months would not fit
in the budget.

### The schema is detected by reading fields

With the date of the change already located, the direct move was to branch on it: one
schema before 9 October, another after. I preferred the pipeline to look at whether the
fields are present in each file and decide from there. If the format changes again, or if
it goes back to the previous one, there is no code to touch and no constant to remember to
update.

The price is more logic in the parser, and that the quality tests have to tolerate, by
stretch, the columns that in the reduced format always arrive null: repository language,
latencies and commit counts. They cannot demand uniform coverage. It struck me as cheap
compared with leaving a magic date written into the code, especially after the provider
had proved that it changes the format without warning.

### Five actor classes

The first version had an `is_bot` column, which is what I had seen in other analyses. With
that boolean my question cannot be answered: an AI agent and a bot that updates
dependencies fall into the same box, and over the two-day test the latter are 202,030
against 58,614 agents.

I classify into `humano`, `bot_ci`, `bot_dependencias`, `agente_ia` and `bot_otro`. Over
the two-day silver test (2025-08-13 and 2026-08-12, 7,719,353 events) the current split is
6,268,266 `humano`, 1,093,101 `bot_ci`, 202,030 `bot_dependencias`, 58,614 `agente_ia` and
97,342 `bot_otro`.

Widening the recognition lists moved 83,922 events out of the catch-all box, which dropped
from 181,264 to 97,342. Of those, 70,601 were CI bots and only 13,321 AI agents: what the
catch-all was absorbing was mostly continuous-integration automation the lists did not yet
recognise. Keeping them up to date is the recurring cost of this decision, and 97,342
events remain that I still cannot name.

### Deduplicating after projecting, not before

The `dropDuplicates` by id was where it seems natural, right at the entrance to silver,
over full bronze. It triggers a shuffle, and that shuffle was dragging along the column
with each event's entire JSON. It filled the system disk; the episode is told below.

I moved it behind the column selection, so the shuffle only moves the id and the fields
that survive into silver. The new order assumes that two rows with the same id are the
same row, because I no longer compare their content. Duplicates are 12,364 out of
1,319,395,759 rows, 0.0009%; in the reconnaissance sample the only one I inspected had
identical content. I have not checked the 12,364 one by one, but at that volume the choice
moves no result.

## What broke and how I fixed it

### The system disk went from 90.5 GB free to 0.5 GB

The silver run aborted with an `IOException` for insufficient space. The full disk was not
the data one but `C:`, where Spark writes its temporary files by default.

The cause was the shuffle of the `dropDuplicates` over full bronze, dragging the whole
JSON along. Cleaning up the orphaned `blockmgr` directories recovered 610.80 GiB, and
581.67 GiB of those were in a single `blockmgr` from a job that had died without releasing
anything.

I moved `spark.local.dir` off the system disk and reordered the deduplication so it
happens after projecting columns. Over the same 7-day test with 24,417,900 bronze rows,
where before it aborted, afterwards it wrote in 112.3 seconds the 2,912,405 `pr_eventos`
rows corresponding to that range.

### Sixteen green tests over a table missing 11 million rows

A silver job died halfway through writing and the quality suite passed in full.

The 16 tests checked internal coherence: types, nulls, ranges, uniqueness of id. All of
that holds just as well in a complete table as in a truncated one. None of them looked at
the volume against the source. 10,979,227 rows were missing, 0.83% of the total, and the
pipeline was signalling success.

I added a reconciliation of counts between bronze and silver by partition, with a
threshold of 0.1%. That 0.83% deviation would have come out eight times above the
threshold. The other 16 tests were well written; what was missing was a check nobody had
written.

### Six days with 588 events per hour instead of the usual 150,000

When processing October 2025 the volume collapsed. Between the 9th and the 14th the hourly
files carried between 588 and 1,346 events, against the roughly 150,000 expected, and did
not recover the normal level until the 15th.

It was not a failure of my download. GH Archive trimmed the payload on the 9th without
announcing it, and on top of that those six days arrive almost empty. I found out by
inspecting files by hand and narrowed the date down by bisection, with a resolution of one
day. There are signs that the real cut happened halfway through the 8th — it has 29% fewer
events than the 7th — but I did not go down to the specific hour because it changed no
decision.

The pipeline reads both formats with no calendar branches, and the six days are excluded
explicitly as a known gap: they are absent data, not scarce data, and averaging them would
have dirtied any time series crossing them.

### A disk estimate wrong by a factor of four

I calculated the weight of the reduced-format stretch by measuring a single day: 0.093
GiB/day. With a sample spread across the months the real figure turned out to be 0.370
GiB/day.

It was not the first time. Earlier I had projected 940 GiB for a year by extrapolating
from a single peak hour; measuring a full day gave 734 GiB, 28% less. The window of the
full-format stretch was revised four times, going through 92, 130, 116 and finally 55
days. Three of those revisions come from the same mistake, extrapolating from a single
sample; the fourth, from provisioning the size of silver by eye with 15% instead of
measuring it.

The fix was not in the code but in the method: set the budget per decision and put a hard
stop in `backfill.py`, which cuts off at 215 GiB instead of trusting the projection in
force. The history ended up taking 192.8 GiB of the 232.8 GiB budgeted.
